# Evidence base — Google's actual rules for content, pages, and pillar/hub structure

This is the **canonical** reference for what Google has actually said about creating content, individual pages, and pillar/topic-cluster structures. `content-brief`, `create-content`, `content-strategy`, and `site-architecture` should all point here rather than each carrying a partial copy — keyword density, word count, and the scaled-content-abuse material below is shared ground truth across all four.

**How this document is organised**, and why the organisation matters: Google's own official guidance (Part A) is stated in full, in Google's own words, with primary sourcing. Everything with no dedicated Google page — pillar pages, topic clusters, "topical authority" (Part B) — is practitioner interpretation of Google's general principles, clearly labelled as such, because treating it as official guidance is the single most common category error in SEO advice.

---

## Part A — Google's official guidance, in full

### A1. The self-assessment questions (the primary source)

From Google's *Creating Helpful, Reliable, People-First Content* — the canonical page for this entire topic, last updated December 2025. Google frames this as the tool for evaluating whether content will succeed in Search: **"Google's automated ranking systems are designed to prioritize helpful, reliable information that's created to benefit people, and not content that's created to manipulate search engine rankings."**

Google explicitly recommends going beyond self-assessment: *"consider having others you trust but who are unaffiliated with your site provide an honest assessment,"* and for existing sites, auditing traffic drops — *"What pages were most impacted and for what types of searches?"*

#### Content and quality questions (12)

- Does the content provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis?
- Does the content provide a substantial, complete, or comprehensive description of the topic?
- Does the content provide insightful analysis or interesting information that is beyond the obvious?
- If the content draws on other sources, does it avoid simply copying or rewriting those sources, and instead provide substantial additional value and originality?
- Does the main heading or page title provide a descriptive, helpful summary of the content?
- Does the main heading or page title avoid exaggerating or being shocking in nature?
- Is this the sort of page you'd want to bookmark, share with a friend, or recommend?
- Would you expect to see this content in or referenced by a printed magazine, encyclopedia, or book?
- Does the content provide substantial value when compared to other pages in search results?
- Does the content have any spelling or stylistic issues?
- Is the content produced well, or does it appear sloppy or hastily produced?
- Is the content mass-produced by or outsourced to a large number of creators, or spread across a large network of sites, so that individual pages or sites don't get as much attention or care?

#### Expertise questions (4)

- Does the content present information in a way that makes you want to trust it, such as clear sourcing, evidence of the expertise involved, background about the author or the site that publishes it, such as through links to an author page or a site's About page?
- If someone researched the site producing the content, would they come away with an impression that it is well-trusted or widely-recognized as an authority on its topic?
- Is this content written or reviewed by an expert or enthusiast who demonstrably knows the topic well?
- Does the content have any easily-verified factual errors?

#### People-first questions (5) — answering yes is a good sign

- Do you have an existing or intended audience for your business or site that would find the content useful if they came directly to you?
- Does your content clearly demonstrate first-hand expertise and a depth of knowledge (for example, expertise that comes from having actually used a product or service, or visiting a place)?
- Does your site have a primary purpose or focus?
- After reading your content, will someone leave feeling they've learned enough about a topic to help achieve their goal?
- Will someone reading your content leave feeling like they've had a satisfying experience?

#### Search-engine-first warning signs (11) — answering yes to any is a red flag

- Is the content primarily made to attract visits from search engines?
- Are you producing lots of content on many different topics in hopes that some of it might perform well in search results?
- Are you using extensive automation to produce content on many topics?
- Are you mainly summarizing what others have to say without adding much value?
- Are you writing about things simply because they seem trending and not because you'd write about them otherwise for your existing audience?
- Does your content leave readers feeling like they need to search again to get better information from other sources?
- **Are you writing to a particular word count because you've heard or read that Google has a preferred word count? (No, we don't.)**
- Did you decide to enter some niche topic area without any real expertise, but instead mainly because you thought you'd get search traffic?
- Does your content promise to answer a question that actually has no answer, such as suggesting there's a release date for a product, movie, or TV show when one isn't confirmed?
- Are you changing the date of pages to make them seem fresh when the content has not substantially changed?
- **Are you adding a lot of new content or removing a lot of older content primarily because you believe it will help your search rankings overall by somehow making your site seem "fresh"? (No, it won't.)**

The two bolded questions are Google directly and explicitly denying the word-count myth and the freshness-gaming myth, in its own official documentation, with its own parenthetical "no." These are not inferred positions.

→ https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content

### A2. E-E-A-T

Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness. Google's own framing of the four:

> **"Of these aspects, trust is most important. The others contribute to trust, but content doesn't necessarily have to demonstrate all of them."** Some content is helpful because of the experience it demonstrates; other content is helpful because of the expertise it shares.

Two things frequently misstated about E-E-A-T:

- **It is not a ranking factor.** Google's own wording: *"While E-E-A-T itself isn't a specific ranking factor, using a mix of factors that can identify content with good E-E-A-T is useful."* It's a rater concept used to validate whether ranking systems are working, not an input the algorithm scores directly.
- **Author identity alone is not a ranking factor either.** Asked whether using a recognised, authoritative doctor to write medical content makes a measurable SEO difference over a lesser-known one, Mueller said no — *"purely from an SEO point of view it probably doesn't matter either way."* What matters is whether the content itself demonstrates the expertise, not the fame of the byline.

**YMYL** — "Your Money or Your Life" — topics affecting health, financial stability, safety, or societal welfare get *"even more weight"* on E-E-A-T signals. This is where expertise and sourcing requirements tighten the most.

The detailed criteria live in Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines, a public PDF used to train human raters whose feedback validates ranking systems — explicitly **not** a direct input to ranking: *"Rater data is not used directly in our ranking algorithms."*
→ https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/hsw-sqrg.pdf

### A3. Who, How, Why

Google's own framework for evaluating content, introduced directly on the helpful-content page as *"a way to stay on course with what our systems seek to reward."*

**Who (created the content)**
- Is it self-evident to your visitors who authored your content?
- Do pages carry a byline, where one might be expected?
- Do bylines lead to further information about the author, giving background about them and the areas they write about?

**How (the content was created)**
Relevant to product reviews (test methodology, evidence of work) and explicitly to AI-generated content:
- Is the use of automation, including AI-generation, self-evident to visitors through disclosures or in other ways?
- Are you providing background about how automation or AI-generation was used?
- Are you explaining why automation or AI was seen as useful to produce the content?

Google's framing: *"Overall, AI or automation disclosures are useful for content where someone might think 'How was this created?' Consider adding these when it would be reasonably expected."*

**Why (the content was created)** — Google calls this *"perhaps the most important question."*
> *"The 'why' should be that you're creating content primarily to help people... If the 'why' is that you're primarily making content to attract search engine visits, that's not aligned with what our systems seek to reward. If you use automation, including AI-generation, to produce content for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings, that's a violation of our spam policies."*

That last sentence is the direct link between this page and the scaled content abuse policy in A5 — Google is explicit that the two are the same rule stated from two angles.

### A4. AI-generated content — no penalty, purpose-tested

Google's stated position, consistent since a February 2023 blog post co-authored by Danny Sullivan and reaffirmed since: **"Appropriate use of AI or automation is not against our guidelines."** The evaluation criteria are the same Who/How/Why framework applied to any content, *"whether you're using AI-generated content or not."*

There is no AI detection penalty. The test is purpose: automation used to help people is fine; automation used primarily to manipulate rankings is a policy violation regardless of the tool.
→ https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2023/02/google-search-and-ai-content

### A5. Scaled content abuse (the spam policy)

Introduced March 2024, replacing the older "spammy automatically generated content" policy specifically to be method-agnostic. Google's definition:

> *"Many pages [generated] for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users, typically focused on creating large amounts of unoriginal content that provides little to no value to users, no matter how it's created."*

Named examples include generative AI producing many pages without added value, and — critically for translation and content-repurposing work — automated transformations *"like synonymizing, translating, or other obfuscation"* that add little value.

Full treatment, including enforcement history and the August 2025 spam update, is in `create-content/references/evidence-base.md`. This document states the rule; that one covers the policy's operational history.
→ https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies

### A6. Page experience

Google's ranking systems *"look to reward content that provides a good page experience,"* with an explicit warning against tunnel vision: *"Site owners... should not focus on only one or two aspects of page experience. Instead, check if you're providing an overall great page experience across many aspects."* Core Web Vitals are one input among several, not the whole of page experience — full technical treatment lives in the Phase 7 `cwv-measurement` and `rendering-audit` skills.

### A7. What Google does not use as a ranking factor — the confirmed list

Compiled from Google's own statements across official documentation and spokesperson comments, cross-referenced with earlier evidence bases in this catalog so nothing here contradicts what `content-brief` or `create-content` already state:

| Claimed factor | Google's actual position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Word count / length | Not a ranking or quality factor. No preferred length exists | Helpful-content page, direct "(No, we don't.)" |
| Keyword density | No optimal density; explicit no from Mueller across successive years | See `content-brief/references/evidence-base.md` |
| Content freshness via cosmetic date changes | Changing dates without substantive changes doesn't help, and Google says so directly | Helpful-content page, "(No, it won't.)" |
| Folder/URL depth | Not counted; no benefit to an artificially flat structure | See `site-architecture/references/evidence-base.md` |
| Author fame/credentials alone | No quantifiable ranking difference from author prominence, independent of content quality | Mueller, Oct 2021 |
| Domain-level link authority | Google doesn't count links at a domain level as a discrete metric | Mueller, Jan 2022 |
| "Topical authority" as a named score | No official authority score or label exists | Mueller/Reddit exchange, Nov 2023 — see Part B |
| E-E-A-T itself | Not a direct ranking factor; a rater concept correlated with signals that are used | Helpful-content page |

## Part B — Pillar pages and topic clusters: practitioner terminology, used with care

**This entire section describes an SEO industry framework, not a Google framework.** Google has never published a page defining "pillar page," "topic cluster," or "hub and spoke," and does not use these terms in its own documentation. Treat everything below as inference from Google's general principles (comprehensiveness, internal linking, avoiding cannibalisation — all in Part A) applied to a specific content-organisation pattern, not as a rule Google has stated.

This distinction matters operationally: a brief or strategy document should never cite "Google requires pillar pages" as a justification. It can legitimately say "this structure applies Google's comprehensiveness and internal-linking guidance in a specific way."

### B1. What the pattern actually is

Terminology varies by source and is used inconsistently across the industry — treat the following as the common core rather than a strict standard:

- **Pillar / hub page**: a broad page covering a topic at a high level, sometimes standalone and comprehensive in itself, sometimes acting as a router to deeper pages
- **Cluster / spoke pages**: narrower pages exploring one subtopic in depth
- **The connective tissue**: bidirectional internal linking — spokes link up to the pillar, the pillar links down to spokes, closely related spokes link to each other

This maps directly onto the `site-architecture` skill's existing page-type taxonomy (hub/spoke/service/resource), which was built independently from Google's own URL-structure and internal-linking guidance rather than from pillar-page marketing content. Where this section and `site-architecture` overlap, treat `site-architecture` as authoritative for the structural mechanics (URL, depth, breadcrumb) and this section as authoritative for the content-coverage reasoning.

### B2. "Topical authority" — treat the term itself with real caution

This is the most over-claimed concept in the space, worth stating plainly rather than diplomatically.

**Google did not coin the term and has not adopted it.** Asked directly on Reddit whether a manager's insistence on "topical authority" as a formal concept was correct, a commenter's rebuttal — that it's a rebranding of ordinary relevance, and that content volume alone doesn't create it — got a one-word reply from John Mueller: **"This."** Followed by explicit advice: *"Don't worry about it."*

Mueller's fuller point, reconstructed from the same exchange: creating substantial, relevant content and organising it sensibly is not a new technique with a name — it's ordinary practice. There is no official "authority" score, and linking related articles together does not automatically manufacture authority as a distinct, separate signal.

**A genuinely separate piece of evidence exists, and it should be labelled for what it is.** A May 2024 leak of Google's internal API documentation showed attributes including `siteFocusScore` (how focused a site is on one topic) and `siteRadius` (how far individual pages deviate from a site's central theme), alongside embedding-based signals capturing overall thematic representation. **This is leaked internal documentation, not an official Google statement** — it should never be cited to a client as confirmed Google policy, but it is independent evidence that something resembling topical focus is measured internally, consistent with the comprehensiveness and originality principles Google states openly in Part A.

**The working conclusion, and the one to actually use in briefs:** don't promise a client "topical authority" as a deliverable, and don't cite it as a ranking factor. Do apply what it's a rebrand of — comprehensive coverage (A1's "substantial, complete, or comprehensive" question), avoiding cannibalisation (Part A, and `content-audit`'s disposition framework), and internal linking that reflects real conceptual relationships rather than arbitrary cross-promotion.

### B3. Internal linking within a cluster — Google statement plus practitioner numbers

The one piece of this pattern with a direct Google quote behind it: *"Internal linking is super critical for SEO. It's one of the biggest things you can do on a website to guide Google and visitors to the pages that you think are important."* — John Mueller.

Beyond that endorsement of internal linking generally, specific link-count numbers are practitioner convention, not Google guidance, and different sources give different numbers for different things — treat them as two different rulers, not a contradiction:

- **Per contextual link within a spoke article**: roughly 3–5, as a starting point rather than a rule
- **Total links into a pillar page**: pillars naturally accumulate more than a single spoke does, since every spoke plus general navigation can point to it

Full mechanical treatment — anchor text, orphan detection, link equity — belongs to the `internal-linking` skill, not this document.

### B4. Length — the same myth, dressed up for pillar pages specifically

Multiple practitioner sources recommend pillar pages of roughly 3,000–5,000 words, sometimes in the very same document that also says "focus on comprehensiveness over a specific word count" — an internal contradiction worth noticing, because it shows how the word-count myth survives even among sources that know better in principle.

**Apply A1 and A7 without exception here.** A pillar page should be as long as covering its topic comprehensively requires. If that's 1,200 words because the topic is narrow, 1,200 words is correct. If a competitor's "pillar page" hits 4,000 words through repetition and filler, matching its length doesn't reproduce whatever is actually working about it. This is the same correction `content-brief` and `create-content` already apply to individual pages, extended to pillars — the two references should never diverge on this point.

### B5. A live disagreement worth surfacing to a client, not concealing

Some current practitioner commentary argues the classic hub-and-spoke pillar model is becoming less effective — critiquing rigid pillar-centric linking and the practice of mixing commercial and informational intent inside one silo, in favour of intent-based grouping with more selective cross-linking. This is a live methodological disagreement inside the SEO industry, not a Google statement on either side.

**Don't resolve this by picking a side and stating it as fact.** State it as a design choice: strict hub-and-spoke (simpler to build and explain, more predictable link equity flow) versus intent-based clustering with selective crosslinks (better matches distinct funnel stages, more work to maintain). `site-architecture` already takes a position compatible with the second — Step 5's cluster ownership model plus explicit permission for cross-cluster linking where genuinely relevant, rather than a strict no-cross-link silo. That's the position this catalog uses; say so, and say why, rather than presenting it as Google's own preference.
